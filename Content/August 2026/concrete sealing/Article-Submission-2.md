## Understanding What Epoxy Flooring Is

Epoxy is a two-part resin system consisting of a base component and a hardener. When the two parts are mixed together, a chemical reaction occurs that causes the material to cure into a hard, rigid coating. Applied over properly prepared concrete, epoxy bonds to the substrate and forms a seamless, impermeable surface layer.

The properties of the cured coating, including its hardness, chemical resistance, flexibility, and thickness, depend on the specific epoxy formulation used. High-build epoxy systems used in industrial environments are formulated for maximum mechanical strength and resistance to heavy traffic and forklift impact. Thin-film epoxy systems used in residential garages or retail spaces are formulated for a smooth, decorative finish with good general durability.

## Commercial and Industrial Epoxy Applications

In commercial and industrial settings, epoxy flooring is valued primarily for its functional properties. Warehouses, food production facilities, commercial kitchens, workshops, and manufacturing environments all use epoxy coatings to create floors that can withstand heavy use, resist chemical contamination, and be cleaned efficiently.

In food-related environments, epoxy systems are specified for their seamless, non-porous surface that prevents bacterial accumulation and can withstand the aggressive cleaning chemicals and high-pressure washdowns used in food production. The floor can be installed with coved skirting details that eliminate the joint between the floor and the wall, further improving hygiene performance.

In industrial environments, anti-slip aggregates are frequently incorporated into the top coat or broadcast into the wet epoxy surface to achieve the slip resistance rating required under health and safety regulations. Quartz or aluminium oxide aggregates are commonly used for this purpose.

## Residential Epoxy Flooring: Garages and Utility Areas

Epoxy flooring in a residential context is most commonly applied in garages, laundries, and utility rooms. In a garage, an epoxy floor provides a durable, easy-to-clean surface that resists oil stains, tyre marks, and the abrasion from grit tracked in by vehicles and foot traffic.

Decorative epoxy systems for garages often incorporate coloured flake or metallic pigment broadcast into the wet coating. These decorative elements add visual interest and also help to conceal minor surface imperfections in the concrete. The finished result is a floor that looks significantly more finished and professional than bare or painted concrete.

---

## Topcoat Options and Their Role in Performance

The base epoxy coating is rarely the final layer in a well-specified floor system. In most commercial and industrial applications, a polyurethane or polyaspartic topcoat is applied over the cured epoxy. This topcoat provides additional UV resistance, which prevents the epoxy from yellowing in areas exposed to natural light. It also adds an extra layer of abrasion resistance and makes the floor easier to clean by providing a smoother, harder surface.

Polyaspartic topcoats in particular have become popular because they cure rapidly, reducing the downtime between installation and the floor being available for use. In commercial environments where every hour of closure represents lost revenue, a fast-curing topcoat can significantly reduce the impact of floor installation on operations. The choice of topcoat should be discussed with the contractor at the specification stage, as different products suit different environments and use cases.

---

## Surface Preparation: The Factor That Determines Performance

No epoxy system performs as intended over poorly prepared concrete. Contamination from oil, grease, dust, or previously applied coatings will prevent the epoxy from bonding to the substrate. Laitance, the weak surface layer that forms as concrete cures, must be removed mechanically before application.

The standard preparation method for epoxy flooring is diamond grinding or shot blasting to achieve the appropriate CSP profile. The profile creates a mechanical key that the epoxy bonds to. A contractor who rushes or skips this step will produce a floor that peels or delaminates, often within months of installation.
